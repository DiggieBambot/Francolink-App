The TCF Canada listening test is 39 multiple-choice questions in 35 minutes, each with four options and one correct answer. Every recording is played once only, the question comes after you have heard it, and the questions get harder as the test goes on. NCLC 7 needs a score of at least 458, and the [TCF score chart](/guides/tcf-canada/score-chart) has every level.

The one-play rule is what makes this part different from everything else you have practised. You cannot rewind, and you cannot read the question first and listen for the answer.

## What is in the TCF Canada listening test?

The recordings run from everyday exchanges up to talks on abstract subjects. [France Éducation International](https://www.france-education-international.fr/test/tcf-canada?langue=fr) lists what the questions test, in rising order of difficulty:

- familiar words and common expressions in everyday situations: dialogues, interviews, phone calls;
- the main point of simple, clear messages and announcements;
- information about people, facts or events in radio or television programmes on current affairs, personal or work topics;
- talks on concrete or abstract subjects;
- any kind of speech at normal speed.

That last line is the important one. The top of the test is French as it is actually spoken, not as it is spoken to learners.

## How is the listening test scored?

You get a score, which France Éducation International converts to a CEFR level and IRCC converts to an NCLC level. For Express Entry, IRCC says to use the score in the column marked "Équivalence ancien score" on your certificate, not the "Score / 699" column. The ranges below are on that scale.

| Score | NCLC |
| --- | --- |
| 331–368 | 4 |
| 369–397 | 5 |
| 398–457 | 6 |
| 458–502 | 7 |
| 503–522 | 8 |
| 523–548 | 9 |
| 549–699 | 10 and above |

Source: IRCC, [TCF Canada equivalency chart](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/immigrate-canada/express-entry/documents/language-requirements/language-testing.html).

Listening is one of the two skills that count for citizenship. Applicants aged 18 to 54 need CLB 4 in [speaking and listening](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/canadian-citizenship/become-canadian-citizen/eligibility/language-proof.html), which is 331 on this scale.

## Why is one-play listening so hard?

Because most listening practice lets you replay, and replaying trains a different skill. With a replay you can decode the recording word by word. With one play you have to understand as it goes by, which means recognising whole phrases at speed rather than building them from individual words.

This is also why France Éducation International's advice for the test day is to stay as focused as possible during the listening part. A few seconds of attention lost on one recording is an answer you cannot recover.

## Should you take notes during the listening test?

No. France Éducation International's own advice is not to lose time taking notes in the multiple-choice parts. Writing pulls your attention off the next sentence, and in a one-play test that is the sentence the question may be about. Listen for the gist instead: who is speaking, where they are, what they want, and whether they are for or against.

## How should you handle the easy questions at the start?

Answer them quickly and confidently, and do not second-guess them. The test is built on rising difficulty, so the early recordings are meant to be easier. The risk at the start is not the French, it is overthinking an easy answer and arriving tired at the hard end of the test.

Answer every question. A blank answer cannot be right.

## What makes French hard to follow when spoken?

Mostly that spoken French joins words together and drops sounds that written French keeps. Three features catch English speakers out:

- **Liaison and enchaînement.** *Les amis* is heard as one word, *lézami*. The word boundary you expect is not there.
- **Dropped *ne*.** In everyday speech *je ne sais pas* is often *je sais pas*, and at speed *chais pas*. The negation is carried by *pas* alone.
- **Silent endings.** *Il parle* and *ils parlent* sound identical. You hear the difference in the context or the pronoun, not the verb.

None of these is exotic. They are how French sounds at the "normal speed" the top of the test uses, so they have to be familiar before the day.

## How do you train for TCF Canada compréhension orale?

Listen every day to real French at natural speed, once through, and then check what you understood. The order matters: one play first, as in the test, and only then the transcript or a replay to see what you missed.

- **Start at your level, not the test's top.** Audio that is far too hard teaches nothing. Material at your level, gradually sped up, builds the phrase recognition the test rewards. Our self-study library is graded from A1 to C2, so you can work at the level just above yours.
- **Use news and radio for the upper end.** The test's later questions draw on current-affairs broadcasts and talks, so that is the register to get used to.
- **Use the official-style practice.** France Éducation International points candidates to [RFI's Français facile](https://francaisfacile.rfi.fr/fr/dipl%C3%B4mes-tests/) for listening practice and to [TV5Monde's TCF training](https://apprendre.tv5monde.com/fr/tcf) for all the compulsory parts.
- **Time a full TCF listening practice test.** 39 questions in 35 minutes, one play each, no notes. Do it at least once before the real thing so the pace is not a surprise.

Speaking practice with a real person trains this too, because conversation is one-play listening by definition.
