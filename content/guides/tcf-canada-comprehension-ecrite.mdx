TCF compréhension écrite, the TCF Canada reading test, is 39 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes, each with four options and one correct answer. The texts start as short everyday documents and end as long, complex articles, and the questions get harder as you go. NCLC 7 needs a score of at least 453, and the [TCF score chart](/guides/tcf-canada/score-chart) has every level.

The average is just over a minute and a half per question, but the time is not needed evenly. The whole skill of this part is spending it where the hard questions are.

## What kinds of texts are in the TCF Canada reading test?

They run from notices and short messages to long specialist articles. [France Éducation International](https://www.france-education-international.fr/test/tcf-canada?langue=fr) lists what the questions test, in rising order of difficulty:

- familiar names, words and very simple sentences in everyday messages, and friendly or administrative letters;
- information in everyday documents: small ads, leaflets, menus, timetables;
- information about people, facts or events in personal letters;
- everyday-language texts about daily life or work;
- articles and reports in which the writer takes a position on a concrete or abstract subject;
- long, complex factual or literary texts and specialist articles;
- abstract or complex extracts from books and specialist articles.

The first half of the test is about finding information. The second half is about understanding a writer's position and the reasoning behind it.

## How is the reading test scored?

You get a score, which France Éducation International converts to a CEFR level and IRCC converts to an NCLC level. For Express Entry, IRCC says to use the score in the column marked "Équivalence ancien score" on your certificate, not the "Score / 699" column. The ranges below are on that scale.

| Score | NCLC |
| --- | --- |
| 342–374 | 4 |
| 375–405 | 5 |
| 406–452 | 6 |
| 453–498 | 7 |
| 499–523 | 8 |
| 524–548 | 9 |
| 549–699 | 10 and above |

Source: IRCC, [TCF Canada equivalency chart](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/immigrate-canada/express-entry/documents/language-requirements/language-testing.html).

Note that the reading and listening ranges are not the same. NCLC 7 starts at 453 in reading and 458 in listening, so a practice score that is "enough" in one is not automatically enough in the other.

## How should you pace the reading test?

Move fast through the early questions to bank time for the late ones. Sixty minutes over 39 questions is about 92 seconds each on average, but a menu or a timetable question should take a fraction of that, and a question on a long argumentative article can take several minutes to do properly.

A simple way to hold yourself to it: note the time when you reach the halfway question. If you are past the 25-minute mark by then, you are spending too long on the easy half. France Éducation International's own advice is to manage your time in this part, and not to lose time taking notes in the multiple-choice parts.

Answer every question, even the ones you have to guess. A blank answer cannot be right.

## How do you answer the early questions quickly?

Read the question first, then scan the document for the one piece of information it asks for. An ad, a timetable or a notice is not meant to be read from top to bottom. If the question asks when the office closes on Saturday, look for *samedi* and a time, and move on.

## How do you answer the questions on long texts?

Read for the writer's position and how they support it, not for every word. The later texts are argumentative, and the questions tend to be about what the author thinks, why, and how one part of the text relates to another. Three things help:

- **Find the thesis.** It is usually in the first or last paragraph. Once you know the writer's position, most of the rest is support for it.
- **Follow the connectors.** *Cependant*, *en revanche*, *or*, *pourtant* signal a turn. *Donc*, *ainsi*, *c'est pourquoi* signal a conclusion. The answer to a "what does the author think" question often sits right after one of them.
- **Distrust the option that repeats the text.** A wrong option often reuses words from the passage in a way that changes the meaning. The right option usually says the same thing in different words.

## Why do English speakers find French reading easier than listening?

Because written French shares a great deal of vocabulary with English, and on the page those words are recognisable in a way they often are not when spoken. *Gouvernement*, *environnement* and *économique* are easy to read and much harder to catch at speed.

That shared vocabulary has limits. Some words look English and mean something else: *actuellement* is "currently", not "actually", and *éventuellement* is "possibly", not "eventually". At the top of the test, one of these can flip the meaning of the sentence a question depends on.

## How do you train for TCF Canada compréhension écrite?

Read French every day, above all argumentative writing, and time yourself against the test's pace. Knowing every word is not the goal. Reading fast enough to reach the end with time for the hardest texts is.

- **Read opinion pieces.** Editorials and op-eds in the French-language press are close to the later texts in the test: a writer taking a position and arguing it.
- **Practise summarising.** After reading an article, say in one sentence what the writer thinks and why. If you cannot, you read the words without the argument, and that is what the hard questions test.
- **Build vocabulary by level.** A reading score is capped by the words you know. Our self-study library runs from A1 to C2, so you can work at the level just above where you are.
- **Use the official-style practice.** France Éducation International points candidates to [TV5Monde's TCF training](https://apprendre.tv5monde.com/fr/tcf) for the compulsory parts.
- **Time a full TCF reading practice test.** 39 questions, 60 minutes, and check where you were at the halfway question.

Reading also feeds the writing test directly. Tâche 3 of [expression écrite](/guides/tcf-canada/expression-ecrite) asks you to read two short documents and compare their positions, which is this skill used under a clock.
