French tenses are usually taught as a list of about twenty forms, which is true and unhelpful. Eight of them carry ordinary French from beginner to B1, two more finish B2, and the ones that frighten people in grammar books are recognition-only: you need to know them when you read them, not produce them.

Here is the whole inventory, and what each one is actually for.

## How many French tenses do you actually need?

You need eight to hold a normal conversation and write ordinary prose. Two more take you through B2, and two of the famous difficult ones are reading skills rather than speaking skills.

The [Office québécois de la langue française catalogues the French verb system](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/la-grammaire/le-verbe) across the indicative, the subjunctive, the conditional, the imperative and the non-finite forms. Counted as separate forms, that is around twenty things. Counted as things you have to be able to build a sentence with, it is eight.

| Tense | Level | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Présent | A1 | Now, habits, and general truths |
| Futur proche | A1 | What you are going to do shortly |
| Passé composé | A2 | A finished event |
| Imparfait | A2 | The situation around the event |
| Futur simple | A2 | Predictions and plans further out |
| Plus-que-parfait | B1 | An event before the one you are telling |
| Conditionnel présent | B1 | Politeness, and hypotheses |
| Subjonctif présent | B1 | After a trigger: necessity, wanting, doubt |
| Subjonctif passé | B2 | The same, about something already done |
| Conditionnel passé | B2 | Regret, reproach, the unreal past |
| Passé simple | C1 | Recognition only. Literary and news narration |
| Subjonctif imparfait | C1 | Recognition only. You will meet it in novels |

The last two rows are the point. They are in our own syllabus as recognition items, not production items, which means the goal is to understand them on the page and never to conjugate them under pressure.

## Which tenses come first, and in what order?

French tenses arrive in level order: présent and futur proche at A1, then passé composé, imparfait and futur simple at A2. B1 adds plus-que-parfait, conditionnel présent and subjonctif présent, and that is the point where French stops feeling like a set of separate forms.

The order is not arbitrary. Each tense in the list depends on one before it. Futur proche needs the présent, because it is built from *aller* in the present plus an infinitive. Plus-que-parfait needs you to already hear the difference between a finished event and its background. Subjonctif présent needs enough verb forms in your head that a new set of endings is a variation rather than a fresh alphabet.

Skipping ahead is what produces learners who can recite the conditional but cannot order lunch.

## What is the difference between futur proche and futur simple?

Futur proche and futur simple both express the future, and in speech they compete directly. The difference is distance: the futur proche presents something as closer and more settled, the futur simple presents it as further off and more open.

The OQLF is precise about this. The futur proche is [*aller* conjugated in the present plus an infinitive](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/24122/la-grammaire/le-verbe/temps-grammaticaux/futur/le-futur-proche), it "est en concurrence avec le futur simple" in everyday language, and what is expressed in the futur proche is "considéré comme moins hypothétique".

So:

- **Je vais appeler ma mère.** I am going to call my mother. Decided, probably today.
- **J'appellerai ma mère.** I will call my mother. Further out, and less committed.

For a beginner this is liberating, because the futur proche needs no new conjugation at all. Learn *aller* in the present and you can talk about the future on day one. That is why it sits at A1 and the futur simple waits until A2.

## Do you actually need the passé simple?

You do not need the passé simple in order to speak. The passé composé has taken over its work entirely in spoken French, so the passé simple is something you read rather than something you produce.

This is not a shortcut anyone is offering you to be kind. The OQLF states it plainly: [the passé composé "empiète souvent sur les emplois du passé simple; il l'a même remplacé complètement à l'oral"](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/24210/la-grammaire/le-verbe/temps-grammaticaux/passe/generalites-sur-le-passe-simple). Completely replaced it in speech.

You will still meet it constantly in novels, history writing and news prose, almost always in the third person: *il fonda*, *elle partit*, *ils comprirent*. Learn to read those. Do not spend a month drilling the first person singular of a form no French person will use out loud in front of you.

## When does the subjunctive stop being optional?

The subjunctive stops being optional at B1, and not because of a rule about mood. It becomes unavoidable because the expressions you need at that level happen to take it.

*Il faut que*, *je veux que*, *bien que*, *pour que*. These are ordinary sentences about necessity, wanting and concession, and each one drags the subjunctive in behind it. The mood is not the thing you learn; the triggers are. Learn the triggers and the conjugation follows as a small set of endings attached to a stem you already know.

Treating the subjunctive as an advanced abstraction is what makes it hard. It is a B1 item in our curriculum for the same reason it is everywhere in spoken French: you cannot say "I have to" naturally in French without it.

## What is the plus-que-parfait for?

The plus-que-parfait marks one past event that happened before another past event. It is the tense that lets you step backwards inside a story you are already telling.

**Quand je suis arrivé, il était déjà parti.** When I arrived, he had already left. The arriving is the story, the leaving happened before it.

You cannot use this until the two-track distinction is solid, which is why it sits after [passé composé and imparfait](/blog/passe-compose-vs-imparfait) rather than alongside them. If you are still choosing between those two by feel, plus-que-parfait will not stick. That contrast is the load-bearing one in the whole past system, and it is worth getting completely solid before adding a third past form on top.

## Where do learners actually waste time on tenses?

Learners waste time on forms they will never produce, almost always because a textbook presented every tense with equal weight. The syllabus tells you which ones are recognition-only, and most self-study never gets that information.

Two patterns show up repeatedly in our own curriculum planning. The first is drilling the passé simple as though it were a speaking tense, which costs weeks and buys nothing you can use in conversation. The second is the opposite failure: treating the subjunctive as optional and postponing it past B1, when the triggers that require it are ordinary B1 sentences a learner is already trying to say.

Both come from the same missing piece, which is a syllabus that ranks the forms instead of listing them. A tense list sorted alphabetically or by grammatical family tells you nothing about what to learn on Tuesday. A list sorted by level does, and the difference between those two documents is most of what separates a year of progress from a year of study.

## What does this look like as a study order?

Learn the eight production tenses in level order, and treat the last two as reading. Present and futur proche, then the three A2 past and future forms, then the three B1 forms that give you hypothesis and necessity.
