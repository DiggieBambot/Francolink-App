How long it takes to learn French depends almost entirely on hours per week, and the number you have probably seen assumes a schedule nobody reading this is on. The official figure comes from a full-time government course running about forty hours a week.

Divide it by your actual week and the answer changes completely. Here is the arithmetic, and the part of it that cannot honestly be pinned down.

## How long does it take to learn French?

At five hours a week, professional-level French takes roughly four and a half years, and at ten hours a week a little over two. Useful conversational French arrives years before either of those, because the official target is set much higher than most people need.

Everything below follows from one sourced number and then division. Where the arithmetic is mine rather than the source's, this page says so.

## What do the official hours actually assume?

They assume a full-time immersion course, not evenings and weekends. The US State Department's [published schedule](https://www.state.gov/national-foreign-affairs-training-center/foreign-language-training) puts French at 30 weeks, with a typical week of "23 hours per week in class and 17 hours of self-study", which is a forty-hour working week spent entirely on French.

That works out at 690 class hours and roughly 510 hours of directed study, so about 1,200 hours in total. The full breakdown, including why French is the joint slowest language in its category, is in [is French hard to learn](/blog/is-french-hard-to-learn).

Two things follow. The target is Professional Working Proficiency, roughly B2 to C1, which is far beyond what most learners want. And the students are full-time adults with professional teaching and no competing obligations, which is not a fair comparison for anyone studying around a job.

## How long is that at a realistic weekly commitment?

At five hours a week it is about 240 weeks, or four and a half years. The 1,200-hour total does not change with your schedule; only the elapsed time does.

| Hours per week | Time to roughly 1,200 hours |
| --- | --- |
| 3 (about 25 minutes a day) | ~7.7 years |
| 5 (about 45 minutes a day) | ~4.5 years |
| 10 (about 1.5 hours a day) | ~2.3 years |
| 20 (a serious part-time commitment) | ~1.2 years |
| 40 (the FSI schedule) | ~30 weeks |

That table is division, not research. The 1,200-hour figure is derived from the State Department's published schedule; the rows are what it becomes at other weekly commitments, and they assume your hours are as well-directed as a full-time course, which is a generous assumption.

## Why will nobody give you a straight hours-per-level table?

Because no authority publishes one. The State Department publishes a single figure for its own end point, and the Council of Europe defines the levels without attaching hours to them, so any table claiming "A1 in 80 hours, A2 in 180" is someone's estimate presented as a standard.

You will see those tables everywhere and the numbers will not agree with each other, which is the clearest evidence that they are not sourced. That does not make the levels useless. It means the honest way to use them is as an ordered sequence of things you can do, and to measure your position by testing it rather than by counting hours.

## How long before French becomes useful?

Much sooner than the full figure suggests, because usefulness arrives at A2 and the official target is two levels above it. A2 is where you can handle ordinary daily situations: ordering, directions, simple exchanges about familiar things.

In our own syllabus A2 is where the past tenses, object pronouns and the future arrive, which is the point a learner stops assembling isolated phrases and starts producing sentences of their own. If your goal is travel or everyday conversation rather than working in French, you are aiming at a fraction of the 1,200 hours.

## What makes the maths worse than it looks?

Unstructured study, which inflates hours without moving you up a level. The FSI figure assumes every one of those forty weekly hours is directed at the next thing you need, and self-study rarely is.

This is where the hour count and real progress separate. A learner can spend two years on vocabulary apps and podcasts, accumulate several hundred genuine hours, and still not be able to build a past-tense sentence unaided, because nothing in that time was sequenced. Hours are the input. Sequence is what converts them.

## What makes it faster?

Order, and output. Learning grammar in the sequence where each item depends on the last removes most of the re-learning, and producing sentences rather than recognising them is what moves a level.

The single highest-return sequencing decision in French is treating the two past tenses as one contrast rather than two topics months apart. Our curriculum records what happens otherwise, at two different levels: *using passé composé for background description* is a signature error at A2, and it returns at B1 as *all-passé-composé narration*. That is months of re-learning caused purely by the order the two forms were met in.

## How do you know which level you are actually at?

By testing, not by estimating from hours. Self-assessment against level descriptions is unreliable in both directions, and the hour counts above are too rough to place anyone.
