Is French hard to learn? Officially, no: the US State Department puts it in its easiest category of languages for English speakers. Less officially, French sits at the slow end of that category, and the published hour figure leaves out a large part of the work.

Both halves of that are worth knowing before you start, and almost nobody says the second one.

## Is French hard to learn for English speakers?

French is in Category I, the easiest of the State Department's four language bands, described as [languages close to English](https://www.state.gov/national-foreign-affairs-training-center/foreign-language-training). It is also the joint slowest language in that band, so "easy category" and "quick" are not the same claim.

The Foreign Service Institute trains US diplomats and publishes how long its courses take to reach Professional Working Proficiency. Category I runs 24 to 30 weeks. French is listed at 30.

| FSI Category I language | Weeks to Professional Working Proficiency |
| --- | --- |
| Danish, Dutch, Italian, Norwegian, Portuguese, Romanian, Swedish | 24 |
| **French** | **30** |
| Spanish | 30 |

Seven of the nine take 24 weeks. French and Spanish take 30. That is a quarter longer than the rest of the "easy" group, and it is the part that gets dropped when the ranking is summarised as "French is one of the easiest languages to learn".

## How many hours does it actually take to learn French?

The State Department puts Category I at 552 to 690 class hours, and French at the top of that range, so 690 class hours. That figure counts classroom time only, and FSI's own description of a typical week shows it is roughly half the real total.

The same page says a typical week is "23 hours per week in class and 17 hours of self-study". Thirty weeks at 23 class hours is the 690 figure. The same thirty weeks at 17 hours of self-study is about 510 hours more, which nobody quotes.

So the honest number for full-time, professionally taught French is closer to 1,200 hours than to 690. Most pages that cite FSI quote a hours figure between 600 and 750 and stop there, which makes French look roughly twice as fast as the institute's own schedule implies.

None of that means it takes you 1,200 hours to order coffee. Professional Working Proficiency is a high bar, around B2 to C1, and useful French arrives a long way before it. What those hours turn into on an ordinary weekly schedule is worked out in [how long it takes to learn French](/blog/how-long-to-learn-french).

## On a scale of 1 to 10, how hard is it to learn French?

About a 4 for an English speaker, where Spanish is a 4 and Mandarin is a 10. French is genuinely one of the more accessible languages available to you, and its difficulty is concentrated in a few specific places rather than spread evenly.

That number is a judgement, not a measurement, and it is worth being explicit about that because the FSI data does not produce a 1 to 10 scale. What it does produce is a ranking: Category I at 24 to 30 weeks, Category II at about 36 weeks, Category III at about 44 weeks, and the hardest group far beyond that.

## Is French harder than Spanish?

No, and the official data treats them identically: both are Category I and both are listed at 30 weeks. Where they differ is which part is hard, not how much.

Spanish pronunciation is close to phonetic, so beginners sound intelligible quickly and the difficulty arrives later with the subjunctive and the past tenses. French front-loads the opposite problem. The spelling-to-sound relationship takes real work before you can say what you already know how to write.

The practical consequence is that Spanish feels easier for the first few months and French catches up. Judging either language by week six is judging the wrong thing.

## What actually makes French hard for English speakers?

Pronunciation and the gap between spelling and speech, which together account for most of the difficulty. The grammar is demanding in a few specific places rather than uniformly hard.

Four things do most of the damage:

- **Silent letters and liaison.** Words blend into each other, so the sentence you hear does not look like the sentence on the page.
- **Nasal vowels.** Sounds with no English equivalent, which have to be learned rather than approximated.
- **Grammatical gender.** Every noun is masculine or feminine, and that gender spreads to the articles and adjectives around it. The full mechanics are in the [French grammar guide](/guides/french/grammar).
- **Two past tenses where English has one.** Passé composé and imparfait split work that English does with a single simple past, which is the single most common stalling point we see.

## What makes French easier than people expect?

Shared vocabulary and a shared alphabet, which between them remove a large amount of the work required by a non-European language. You start with a passive vocabulary you did not know you had.

Centuries of borrowing mean a great many French words are recognisable on sight to an English reader: *nation*, *possible*, *important*, *restaurant*. There is no new writing system, no tones, and no case endings. Compared with a Category III or IV language, you are starting most of the way up the hill.

## How long before you can actually hold a conversation?

Far sooner than the FSI numbers suggest, because those numbers describe professional proficiency rather than ordinary usefulness. Simple conversation about familiar things is an A2 skill, and A2 is two levels below what FSI is measuring.

This is the most misread thing about the hours data. The 690 class hours buys a diplomat who can work in French, not a traveller who can order dinner and explain what they do for a living. If your goal is the second one, you are aiming at a much nearer target and should measure yourself against the CEFR levels rather than against a State Department course.

## Where do learners actually get stuck?

Not at the start, and not on the hardest material. The stall shows up in the middle, at the point where a rule learned early has become a habit that blocks the next thing.

Our own curriculum records the clearest example twice, at two different levels. *Using passé composé for background description* is a signature error at A2, and it comes back at B1 as *all-passé-composé narration*. Same habit, one level older. It happens because passé composé is taught first, works well enough for months, and becomes the default before the imparfait can compete.

That pattern is what makes difficulty feel unpredictable. The hard part is rarely the new material; it is the earlier material that was learned in the wrong order and now has to be unlearned. A syllabus that sequences the two past tenses as a deliberate contrast, rather than as two separate topics months apart, removes most of it.
